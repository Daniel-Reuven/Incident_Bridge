Using Incident Bridge

Incident Bridge tracks your team's IT work in two queues: routine maintenance tasks, done strictly in order, and faults, where the most severe one always comes first. This guide covers everything a user can do, then the extra tools an admin has. Admin-only steps are marked Admin.

Getting started

  • Your account is created for you by an admin — there is no sign-up. Ask your administrator for a username and a first password.
  • At your first login (and after an admin resets your password) you must choose a new password before you can do anything else — unless the operator turned this off.
  • Log in on the start page. You stay logged in in that browser for up to 12 hours (your administrator may set another limit), or until you press Log out in the top bar. After 5 wrong passwords in a row the account is locked for 15 minutes.
  • Your username and role (user or admin) are shown in the top bar.

Settings: appearance and password

Press Settings in the top bar.

  • Interface — the color scheme (Light, Dark, or System, which follows your computer's setting) and the text size (90% to 130%). They apply at once, to every page, and are saved with your account, so they follow you to any computer — as does your choice of updates order on an incident page. The login page always uses your computer's color scheme; your own settings return as soon as you log in.
  • Password — type your current password, the new one, and the new one again. A checklist under the new password shows which rules it already meets; the i button next to it shows the password policy. Changing your password signs you out on every other computer or browser.

Password policy

Every password — your own, and one an admin sets for you — must:

  • be 8 to 128 characters long;
  • contain at least one uppercase letter, one lowercase letter, one digit and one symbol (anything that is not a letter or a digit, for example ! @ # $ % or a space);
  • not contain your username (in any letter case);
  • not be a common password such as P@ssw0rd or Welcome123! — the app keeps a list of them;
  • when you change it, differ from your current password.

A long passphrase is easier to remember and harder to guess than a short jumble, for example Coffee before 9 deploys!. Passwords are stored only as a salted hash, never as text — nobody, not even an admin, can see yours.

The dashboard

After logging in you land on the dashboard. It has four tabs, each with its count: Everything (all of the below), Maintenance (just the maintenance queue — every task in it is open or in progress, in order, so there is nothing to sort), Faults (the fault queue and the active faults) and Archive (closed incidents of both kinds, most recently closed first). The Everything tab, from top to bottom:

  • Maintenance queue — first in, first out: tasks are done one at a time, in the order they were reported. #1 in queue is next.
  • Fault queue — priority order: every Critical fault before any Major one, and every Major before any Minor; within a severity, oldest first.
  • All incidents — everything, including closed incidents. The Type, Status and Sort by filters at the top apply to this list; the button next to Sort by reverses the order (for example Most severe first / Least severe first).

Type in Filter to show only incidents whose title, description or comments contain the words you type (a row found only through a comment says match in comments); the tab counts then show matching / total.

Times are shown in your own time zone, for example 3 hours ago, 14:36 GMT+3 2026/10/17 — someone in another country sees the same moment in theirs.

Click any row to open the incident. The dashboard updates by itself when anyone changes something — a closed incident moves to Archive on its own — there is no need to refresh. Coming back from an incident page reopens the same tab, with the same filter.

Two helpers

  • Check stale incidents highlights in-progress incidents with no update for the time chosen in Stale after (default 4 hours). Not shown on the Archive tab.
  • Pressing maintenance calls (Maintenance tab) lists open maintenance tasks that have waited more than 3 days, oldest first. Show first limits the list.

Reporting an incident

  1. On the dashboard, press New incident.
  2. Choose the Kind: Maintenance task for planned, routine work, or Fault / issue for something that is broken.
  3. Give it a short Title and a Description of what you see.
  4. For a fault, answer the four severity questions — System is unavailable, Security breach, Performance degraded and Affected users (%). The app sets the severity from your answers:
    • unavailable or a security breach → Critical
    • performance degraded, or at least 25% of users affected → Major
    • anything else → Minor
  5. Press Create. A maintenance task joins the back of the maintenance queue; a fault takes its place in the fault queue by severity.

Working maintenance tasks

  • Start next (dashboard) or Start (on the task's page) begins the first task in line. Tasks start strictly in order, and only one can be in progress at a time.
  • When the work is done, open the task and press Complete, with a short closing message.
  • A task that is no longer needed can be closed from any place in the queue with Close task (a reason is required); the tasks behind it move up one place.

Working faults

  • Claim next (dashboard) takes the most severe fault that is waiting and assigns it to you.
  • On a fault's own page, Claim works for any fault in the most severe group that is waiting. If more severe faults are still waiting, the button is disabled and tells you how many.
  • Post what you find as updates in the box under Updates (up to 5,000 characters; the counter shows how many you have used). Order shows the newest or the oldest first — your browser remembers the choice.
  • When it is fixed, press Resolve and write what was done (up to 1,000 characters). A fault can also be resolved while it is still waiting, without claiming it.
Every status change — claimed, closed, reopened — is written into the incident's updates automatically, with who did it and why, so the history is always complete.

AI analysis of a fault

When the server has an AI provider set up, open and in-progress faults show an Analyze using <provider> button. The AI reads the fault and all its updates and posts its view — a summary, the likely cause, evidence, next steps, missing information and a severity check — as a comment by ai-assistant.

  1. Press Analyze using … — a comment records that you asked, and the button shows the request's place: Queued (position 2), then Analyzing…. Analyses run one at a time, so this can take a few minutes.
  2. While it waits, anyone can press Cancel analysis.
  3. The answer appears in the updates. A long answer is split into up to three parts; if the AI could not answer, an error comment says why.
  • A fault can have only one analysis waiting at a time.
  • After a successful analysis, the button is greyed out (No changes since the last analysis) until someone changes the fault — a new update, a status or severity change — because the answer would be the same.
  • Admin Re-analyze runs it again without changes, once a cooldown has passed since the last analysis (5 minutes by default); the button counts down until then.
The fault and its updates are sent to the AI provider (Google, Anthropic or OpenAI, as configured). Do not put passwords, keys or personal data in incidents — see the Terms.

Admin tools Admin

  • Not an incident and By design close an incident with that reason, instead of Resolve. A closing message is still required.
  • Severity — on an in-progress fault, the Severity menu changes it; the fault's place in the queue follows.
  • Reopen… on a closed incident, with a reason. A maintenance task asks where in the queue it goes back; a fault goes back to the queue (Open) or to you (In progress). The earlier resolution stays in the history.
  • Import seed data (dashboard) loads incidents from a .jsonl file; incidents already there are skipped, not duplicated. Every username in the file (who reported, who is assigned, who wrote each update) must be an account here, or that incident is skipped with the reason. The sample data uses admin, tech1, tech2 and tech3: name the first admin admin at setup and create the three technicians (for example through INCIDENT_BRIDGE_USERS) before importing it.
  • Re-analyze — see AI analysis.

Users Admin

Users in the top bar lists every account.

  • New user — a username (3–32 letters, digits, ., _ or -), a role and a first password that follows the policy. Give the password to the person; they must change it at their first login.
  • Details — first, middle and last name, job title and location, all optional, and how this person's name is shown (below).
  • Make admin / Make user changes the role.
  • Disable signs the person out at once and stops them from logging in; Enable lets them back in. Accounts are never deleted, so their incidents and comments keep their author.
  • Reset password sets a new password for someone who forgot theirs; it signs them out everywhere and they must change it at their next login.
  • You cannot change your own role or disable yourself — use Settings for your own password.

Display names

Everywhere in the app, people are shown by a display name built from their details — in the top bar, as reporter, as the author of updates, in the site portal and in live notices. Display names at the top of the Users page sets the format for everyone: pick one of the ready-made formats, or write your own with the field buttons — {first-name}, {middle-name}, {last-name}, {job-title}, {location} and {username} — for example {first-name} {last-name} - {job-title} ({location}). The example under it shows the result at once. In a person's Details, A format of their own overrides it for that person only.

  • Empty fields leave nothing behind: no empty brackets or stray dashes. With no details at all, the username is shown.
  • The location is shown lighter and smaller, since it matters less than the name.
  • The username stays the person's identity: it is used to log in, it appears under the name on the Users page and when you point at a name, and the activity log records usernames.

Activity log Admin

Logs in the top bar shows what happened in the app: every action (logins, incidents created, claimed, commented, closed, AI analyses, users changed, site-portal changes…), every request to the server, and every call to an outside service such as the AI provider. The newest records come first, one month at a time.

  • Filters — Month, Category (auth, users, incident, site, external, data, setup, http, app), Event (for example incident.claimed), User, Level (Warning shows failed logins, refused requests and failed outside calls) and Search over the whole record. The category starts at Actions, which leaves out the HTTP requests and the data records (below), because they outnumber the actions many times over; choose Everything, http or data to see them. The page remembers your filters until you close the tab.
  • Click a row to see every field of the record. Show older records loads more of the month.
  • Check integrity checks every record of every month. Each record is signed and points to the one before it, and the database remembers how far the log has reached, so a record that was edited, removed, reordered or added by hand — or a whole month that was deleted — is reported, with where it happened. It then checks the database too: every time the app saves something, it writes a fingerprint of it (not its content) to the log as a data record, so anything changed, deleted or added in the database outside the app is reported. No tampering found means the log is complete and unchanged, and the database matches what the app saved.
  • There is no way to edit or delete records in the app, for admins too. The log files are encrypted; the log of an earlier installation is never shown. Times are shown in your time zone.

The site portal Admin

The Site portal button in the top bar watches the organization's websites and tells the right people when one has a problem.

  • Check all sites, or Check on one site, tests whether each site answers. One failed check marks it Degraded; two in a row, Down — so a single dropped request never raises an outage. Status sets a status by hand, with a reason (Maintenance is only set this way).
  • The summary at the top shows the most urgent site and anything that needs attention: outages no incident mentions, sites that look fine although incidents mention them, sites no mailing list covers, lists with no members.
  • Mailing lists say who is told about which sites.
  • Notifications: each meaningful status change creates a draft to the lists covering that site. Open it with Review, edit the text if needed, then Send to lists or Dismiss.
  • Archive removes a site or list from use; the Archive tab can Restore it.
  • Sent, dismissed and skipped notifications move to the Archive tab after a set time (30 days unless the operator changed it), where they can only be viewed. Notifications waiting for a decision stay in Notifications.

Server settings: HTTPS and AI Admin

Server in the top bar sets how the server is reached and which AI provider analyzes faults. The tag at the top shows the server's mode, set by the operator in backend/.env (APP_MODE): Testing or Production.

  • HTTPS — Serve HTTPS adds an encrypted address next to the plain one, on its own port (for example https://your-server:8443). HTTPS only for remote connections sends anyone on another computer who uses the plain address on to the HTTPS one. From the server computer itself the plain address always keeps working, so a wrong setting can never lock you out.
  • Certificate — while testing, the app can make its own (self-signed): each browser warns about it once, then lets you continue. In production it must be a certificate from a certificate authority — your organization, your hosting company, or a public one such as Let's Encrypt, DigiCert or GoDaddy. Enter the paths of its certificate file (with the intermediate certificates after it, as the authority provides them) and its key file, and Check the files. In production the server will not start without HTTPS, a valid certificate from an authority, and HTTPS only for remote connections.
  • Trusted proxies — only when another server (a reverse proxy) sits in front of the app: its address, so the app believes what it reports about the visitor. Leave it empty otherwise.
  • Each field shows where its value comes from: set here, from .env or default. Values saved here win over .env; Use .env instead removes them. These settings take effect when the server starts: the page says Restart the server to apply until then.
  • AI analysis — the provider, its API key and models, as at setup; Off turns the analysis off, Use the .env settings goes back to the operator's. Saved changes apply at once. The key is stored encrypted and never shown again; leave the field empty to keep it.
A login made at the HTTPS address is never sent back over the plain one. In production, browsers are also told to use only HTTPS for this server from then on.

First-time setup and the recovery key Admin

A new installation shows only its setup page until it is finished; every other page leads there.

  1. Setup code — the server prints it in its console window when it starts. Only the person running the server can see it, so nobody else can take over a fresh installation. The page then checks the folders the app will use — by default backend/instance for the data and backend/instance/logs for the activity log. Each must be empty or not exist yet: the app keeps its folders to itself. If one holds anything, the page shows what is in it; empty it and press Check again, or give the app another folder in backend/.env (DATA_DIR, LOG_DIR) and restart the server.
  2. Recovery key — all data is encrypted. The computer keeps the working key in its own protected store (Windows Credential Manager, macOS Keychain, or the Linux equivalent), so the app starts by itself. The recovery key is the only other way in: on a new computer, or if that key is lost. It is shown only once. Copy or download it, keep it offline and away from the server (a password manager, a printout in a safe), keep a backup copy, and type its last two groups to confirm.
  3. Admin account — the first admin, with a password that follows the policy, and optionally your name and job title (for your display name). More admins are added later on the Users page.
  4. AI analysis (optional) — the provider and its API key; Test connection checks the key. The key is stored encrypted with the data.

Regular users that the operator listed in the server's settings are created at the same time and choose their own password at their first login.

Moving the data to another folder

The data folder holds the database, its keys file, the activity log and the generated HTTPS certificate, each marked as belonging to this installation; the app refuses to start if a folder in its settings belongs to something else. To move it:

  1. Stop the server — it keeps the database and the log open (the tool refuses while it runs).
  2. In the backend folder run python move_data.py D:\IncidentBridge\data — any new folder that is empty or does not exist yet. Add --logs <folder> to keep the activity log in a folder of its own.
  3. The tool copies everything, checks that the copy opens as the same installation and that every log line can be read, and prints the lines to put in backend/.env (for example DATA_DIR=D:\IncidentBridge\data).
  4. Put them in .env, start the server and check that everything is there. The old folder is left untouched: delete it yourself once you are sure.
Moving to a new computer, or the computer's key store was lost? With the server stopped, run python manage_keys.py restore in the backend folder and enter the recovery key. If the recovery key may have been seen by someone else, python manage_keys.py new-recovery-key replaces it and re-encrypts the data.

What is recorded and stored

The activity log (files on the server, encrypted, one per month; finished months are compressed and kept until the operator deletes them) records, for each event:

  • the time, the event and its level, the username, and the network address it came from — by default only as a code that cannot be turned back into the address (the same address always gets the same code), or as the operator chose: its last part only (***.***.***.1), in full, or not at all;
  • what was done, the ids of what it concerns (incident, user, site, list…) and incident and site titles;
  • for a request to the server: the method, path, query, status code and how long it took;
  • for a call to an outside service (AI provider, site check): the service, model, address, status, outcome and duration.
  • for every database record the app saves or deletes: its kind, its id and a fingerprint — never its content.

It never records passwords (not even wrong ones), API keys, cookies, request or reply bodies, incident descriptions, the text of updates, or what was sent to the AI provider.

The database (one file on the server, encrypted — unreadable without its key, see setup) stores accounts (username, role, the optional name, job title and location, a salted hash of the password — never the password — and its interface settings (color scheme, text size, updates order), whether the account is disabled or must change its password), incidents with their details, updates and history, sites, mailing lists (with email addresses) and notifications, the AI analysis queue and its results, and the activity log's checkpoint.

Your browser keeps the session cookie that keeps you logged in, a copy of your interface settings while you are logged in (deleted when you log out), and, until you close the tab, the tab's id and your dashboard and log filters. See also the Terms.

Good to know

  • Course requirements: the Requirements page (any logged-in user) lists every requirement of the course brief, with the code that meets it, where the app uses it, and a Run button that shows it working on sample data held in memory — your data is never touched.
  • Live updates: lists update by themselves. If someone changes the incident you are looking at, a prompt offers to refresh it.
  • Server restarts: open pages reconnect on their own within a few seconds, and you stay logged in.
  • Something refused? The message says why — for example, a task that is not first in line cannot be started, and admin-only actions are not offered to users.
